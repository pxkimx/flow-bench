Tutorial

From raw files to a figure and a table

This page takes you from the files your cytometer wrote to gates you can defend, a graph and a table. It assumes you have never analysed flow data. Each step says what to do, where to click, and why.

1. Before you begin: what should be in your folder

A flow cytometer measures one cell at a time as it passes a laser, and writes one FCS file per tube or well. Each row in the file is one event (usually one cell) and each column is one measurement: how much light the cell scattered, and how bright it was in each colour.

The tubes you want to study are not enough by themselves. Controls tell you where "negative" ends and "positive" begins.

TubeWhat is in itThe question it answers
Study samplesYour cells with every stainWhat you want to know
UnstainedThe same cells, no stainHow bright are the cells with nothing added?
Single-stain, one per colourCells or beads with one stain onlyHow much of this colour leaks into the other detectors?
FMO (fluorescence minus one)Every stain except oneWhere does negative end for the missing colour, once all the others are there?
Viability dye, in every tubeA dye only dead cells take upWhich cells were dead?
Why it matters. A gate drawn without a control is a guess. If you did not run a control, you can still analyse the data, but Flow Bench will say so in its checks and you should say so too [1, 4].

One colour only, such as a single GFP reporter? You need the study samples and a tube of the same cells without the reporter. You can skip compensation.

2. Load the files and tell Flow Bench what each one is

In Flow Bench
  1. On the tab, press Add folder and choose the folder, or drag the files onto the window. Nothing is copied to the internet and your files are never changed.
  2. Open the tab. Each file has a Role. Flow Bench guesses it from the file name; correct any that are wrong. For a single-stain or FMO control, also pick its Stained channel.
  3. Give each study sample a Group: the condition it belongs to, such as Control or Treated. Use exactly the same spelling for files in the same group.
  4. Untick Use for a file you want to keep out of the statistics, such as a failed tube.
Why groups matter. The statistics and the Prism tables are built from groups. Each file in a group counts as one replicate: one animal, one culture, one donor.

3. How to read a plot

A population is a cloud of cells that sit together. A gate is a shape you draw around a cloud to say "these cells". The cells inside become a new population that you can plot and gate again. Analysis is a short chain of gates, each one removing something you do not want, until the last gate holds the cells you care about.

The name on each axis is a button. Click it to change what the axis shows.

4. First gate: find the cells

In Flow Bench
  1. The first plot is already there: FSC-A against SSC-A, all events.
  2. On the tab choose Polygon. Click around the main cloud, one corner at a time. Click the first corner again to close it. Press Esc to start over.
  3. Name it Cells.
Why a loose gate. This gate has one job: remove debris and the events stuck at the edges of the plot. Draw it generously. A tight scatter gate can throw away the very cells you are studying, because activated, dividing or dying cells change size [1]. Leave the fine decisions to the later gates.

5. Second gate: single cells only

In Flow Bench
  1. Click the Cells gate, then Open plot (or double-click the gate). A new plot shows only the cells inside it.
  2. If its axes are not already FSC-A and FSC-H, click the axis names and choose them.
  3. Draw a Polygon along the diagonal. Name it Singlets.
Why it matters. Two cells passing together are read as one cell with the colours of both. A green cell stuck to a red cell looks like a cell that is both, which does not exist. Doublets also look like cells with twice the DNA [7].

No FSC-H in your files? Use FSC-A against FSC-W, or SSC-A against SSC-H. The idea is the same.

6. Third gate: living cells, if you used a viability dye

In Flow Bench
  1. Click the Singlets gate. On the tab press Histogram, then click the axis name and choose the channel of your viability dye.
  2. On the Gating tab choose Range and drag across the dim peak. Name it Live.
Why it matters. Dead cells soak up antibodies and glow in many colours at once. Left in, they show up as false positives in almost every gate that follows [1]. If you did not use a viability dye, skip this step and say so when you report the result.

7. Compensation: taking one colour out of another's detector

No dye glows in a single pure colour. Some of the light from one dye lands in the detector meant for another. This is spillover. Compensation subtracts it, using the single-stain controls to measure how much to subtract [2].

Your situationChoose, on the tabWhy
Compensation was set up on the cytometer when the tubes were runMatrix in each fileThe cytometer stored its matrix in every file. This is the usual case.
It was not, but you ran single-stain controlsFrom controlsFlow Bench works the matrix out from the controls you marked on the Data tab.
The stored matrix is nearly right but one pair is offCopy file matrix, then click that cell and type a new valueYou keep the cytometer's matrix and correct one number. Do this only with the single-stain control in front of you.
One colour only, or colours that do not overlapNoneThere is nothing to subtract.
Check it
  1. On the Compensation tab, click a cell of the matrix. Two plots appear for that pair of colours: as measured, and compensated.
  2. Look at the single-stain control. In the compensated plot its bright cells should be level with its dim cells.
  3. If the bright cells still climb, the pair is under-compensated. If they dip below the dim cells, it is over-compensated. Flow Bench lists pairs that are off and by how much.
Why it matters. Without compensation, cells that are bright in one colour look positive in the next one too. A wrong matrix does the same thing more quietly. Compensation does not remove the spreading of a bright population; that is why FMO controls are still needed (step 9) [2, 4].

8. Scales: why fluorescence is not drawn on a ruler

ScaleUse it forWhy
LinearScatter (FSC, SSC), time, DNA contentThese do not span a huge range, and a doubling should look like a doubling.
Logicle (Flow Bench's choice for fluorescence)Antibody stains and reportersBright cells can be ten thousand times brighter than dim ones, so the top is drawn like a log scale. Near zero it is drawn like a ruler, so cells at or below zero stay visible [3].
LogRarelyIt cannot show zero or negative values, which compensated data always has.
ArcsinhIf your lab already uses itMuch like logicle, with different settings.
In Flow Bench
  1. Click an axis name. The lower part of the menu sets the scale for that channel on every plot.
  2. If the dim cloud is cut off at the left or bottom edge, raise the Logicle width (W) one step at a time until the whole cloud shows.
Why negative numbers are normal. Compensation is a subtraction, and every measurement has some noise. Subtract from a number near zero and some cells land a little below zero. They are real cells and belong in the negative population [3, 6]. Changing the scale never changes a count: a gate keeps the same cells whichever scale you look through.

9. Deciding what is positive

This is the gate that answers your question, so it needs the most care. Do not place it by eye on the stained sample alone. Place it against a control.

In Flow Bench
  1. Click the population you want to split (Live, or Singlets). Press Insert then Histogram and choose the marker's channel.
  2. If you marked an FMO control for that channel on the Data tab, it is drawn behind as a dashed line. Without one, the unstained control is used.
  3. Choose Range and drag from the point where the dashed line has fallen away, out to the right edge. Name it after the marker, for example CD4+.
ShapeUse it when
RangeOne marker, positive or negative. The simplest and the easiest to defend.
QuadrantTwo markers at once, each simply on or off. One click places the cross; you get four populations.
RectangleA cloud that sits square to the axes.
PolygonA cloud that is slanted or oddly shaped, where a straight cut would slice through it.
Look back to check the gate
  1. Click your new gate and press Open plot. Set the new plot's axes to FSC-A and SSC-A.
  2. The cells in your gate should sit where your cells sat in the first plot. If many sit in the debris corner or far off to one side, the gate is catching something that is not your population.
Why an FMO and not just unstained cells. With many colours on the same cell, the bright ones widen the spread of the others even after compensation. An unstained tube cannot show that; an FMO tube can, because it has every other colour in it [4]. For a bright, well separated marker the difference is small. For a dim marker, or one that forms a smear, it can change the answer.

10. One gate for every file

A gate you draw applies to all files. Press Next (or Page Down) and look at each file to see that the gate still fits.

"Apply changes to" on the Gating tabWhat moving a gate doesChoose it when
All filesMoves it everywhereAlmost always. Start here.
GroupMoves it for the files in this file's groupA whole group was run on another day or with other settings.
This fileMoves it for this file onlyOne tube shifted, for example a different cell number changed the scatter.
Why the same gate. If each file gets its own gate, you can move the answer wherever you like without meaning to. Use one gate unless there is a reason you could explain to a reviewer. When a file does have its own gate, Flow Bench marks it, lists it in the checks and prints it in the report. Use shared puts a file back on the common gate.

11. Read the checks

The Checks panel on the right looks for the common ways an analysis goes wrong. A warning is a prompt to look, not a verdict.

WarningWhat it meansWhat to do
Compensation looks offA single-stain control still shows signal in another detectorOpen the Compensation tab and look at that pair. A dim control gives a rough estimate, so judge by eye too.
Gate reaches into the FMO controlCells that cannot be positive fall inside the gateMove the gate's lower edge up.
No FMO controlThe gate was placed without oneAcceptable for bright, well separated markers. Say so in your methods.
Only a few events in a gateThe percentage rests on very few cellsRead the interval it gives. Collect more cells next time.
Flow rate was unevenA clog or an air bubble during the runPlot Time against a scatter channel and, if needed, gate out the bad stretch.
Events at the detector maximumSome cells were too bright to measureThey pile up at the top edge. Keep them out of intensity measurements; lower the voltage next time.
Different voltagesFiles were recorded with different settingsPercentages may still be comparable. Intensities are not.
A file has its own gateSee step 10Be ready to explain why.

The limits behind these warnings are starting values. Set them to what your lab accepts on the tab. They are printed in every report.

12. Which number to report

NumberUse it to sayExample
% of parentHow common a population is within the gate before it"32% of live cells were CD4+"
% of all eventsRarely the right choice: it also counts debris and dead cells
EventsHow many cells the percentage rests onAlways worth a look
Median intensityHow much of a marker the cells carry, when all cells shift together"Median PD-1 doubled"
Geometric meanThe same, in labs that use it by habit

Every gate gets a percent of parent for every file automatically. To add a median, go to the tab and use Add a statistic.

Percent or intensity? If the plot shows two clouds, positive and negative, report a percentage. If it shows one cloud that slides brighter or dimmer, report the median. Why the median and not the mean: a handful of extremely bright cells can drag the mean a long way; the median barely moves [1].
How many cells is enough? Counting is noisy. A gate with 100 cells in it is uncertain by about 10%, one with 10,000 cells by about 1% (the square root of the count, as a share of the count). For a rare population, collect enough events that the gate ends up with a few hundred cells.

13. Comparing groups

On the Statistics tab, each file gives one value. Groups are compared file against file.

Why not cell against cell. The cells in one tube all came from the same animal or dish. They are not independent, so thousands of them do not make thousands of replicates. Your replicates are the animals, cultures or donors [5].
Your designTests shownWhich to read
Two groupsWelch's t-test and Mann–WhitneyThe t-test compares means and suits values that are roughly bell shaped. Mann–Whitney compares ranks and makes fewer assumptions, but cannot give a small p-value with very few files.
Three or more groupsOne-way ANOVA and Kruskal–WallisEach asks whether any group differs. Neither says which one. For that, send the table to Prism and run a multiple-comparison test there.

With fewer than three files in a group, no test is trustworthy. Show the dots and say how many there are. Flow Bench does not correct for testing many statistics at once.

14. Make the figure and the tables

The page of plots
  1. The layout page is your figure in the making. On the Insert tab add a Density plot or Histogram of any population, a Statistics table, or a Chart of one number by group.
  2. A common figure shows the gating path once, for one typical file: Cells, Singlets, Live, then the marker.
On the Export tab
  1. PDF report: the settings, the checks, the statistics, and every plot of every study file. Keep it with your notebook.
  2. Excel: one row per file with every number, plus every gate in every file.
  3. GraphPad Prism: press New Prism file. You get one table per statistic, with your groups as columns. Make your graphs in Prism and save. If you change a gate later, come back and press Update Prism: the numbers in that file are replaced and your graphs redraw when Prism opens it. A dated backup is kept each time. Close the file in Prism before updating.
  4. Gating-ML: the gates in a standard format, for a methods section or other software.
  5. Save the project so you can reopen the analysis as you left it.

15. A ten-minute walk-through on real data

The example is published data: chicken cells carrying a cell-cycle reporter, grown in three conditions, three cell lines each (Sudderick et al., PLoS Biology 2026). It opens with four starter gates drawn by Flow Bench. They are a place to start, not the authors' gates.

  1. . Look at the Data tab: nine files in three groups, and one tube of cells without the reporter marked as the unstained control.
  2. On the Home tab, press Next a few times. Every plot follows the file. Watch the third plot change between conditions.
  3. Go to Gating. Click the Cells gate on the first plot and drag one corner. The numbers, the gate view and the chart all follow.
  4. Click the name on the histogram's axis and switch its scale between Linear and Logicle. The two peaks sit a factor of two apart, which a linear scale shows best.
  5. Draw a gate of your own: choose Range, drag across one peak of the histogram, and name it.
  6. Open Statistics. Your new gate is in the list. Click it to see one dot per file and the group comparison.
  7. Open Export and press PDF report to see what you would hand to a colleague.

16. Eight common mistakes

  1. Gating scatter so tightly that real cells are thrown out.
  2. Skipping the singlet gate, then finding "double-positive" cells that are two cells stuck together.
  3. Leaving dead cells in.
  4. Placing a positive gate by eye, with no control behind it.
  5. Using an unstained tube where a dim marker needs an FMO.
  6. Giving every file its own gate.
  7. Reporting a percentage that rests on a dozen cells.
  8. Counting cells as replicates.

17. Words you will meet

Event
One thing the cytometer measured: usually a cell, sometimes debris or two cells together.
Channel, detector
One measurement. Names such as B 530/30-A give the laser (B, blue), the filter (530/30) and the pulse measure (A, area).
FSC, SSC
Forward and side scatter: light bounced off the cell. Roughly size, and internal complexity.
A, H, W
Area, height and width of the light pulse from one cell.
Population
The cells inside a gate.
Parent
The population a gate was drawn on. "Percent of parent" is a share of that.
Spillover
Light from one dye landing in another dye's detector.
Compensation
Subtracting spillover.
FMO
A control with every stain except one.
MFI
"Mean" or "median fluorescence intensity". Say which. Flow Bench reports the median.
Logicle, biexponential
A scale that is like a ruler near zero and like a log scale higher up.
